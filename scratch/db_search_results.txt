  ID: 12 | Value: One of the largest book fairs in the Arab world, SILA draws publishers, authors, and readers from across Algeria and the Francophone world to the Palais des Expositions. Hundreds of thousands of visitors browse thousands of titles, attend literary panels, and discover emerging Algerian writers over two vibrant weeks.
  ID: 13 | Value: A week-long open-air festival celebrating Algerian music, theatre, and crafts. Stages are set up along the waterfront corniche and in public squares, featuring raï, chaabi, and contemporary Algerian artists performing free concerts every evening. Food stalls serve traditional Algerian street food under string lights by the sea.
  ID: 14 | Value: A celebration of raï — the genre born in the streets of Oran — this three-day international festival transforms the city into a giant concert venue. Artists from Algeria, France, Morocco, and beyond perform across multiple outdoor stages in the city center. The festival draws tens of thousands of fans and ends with a massive free concert at the seafront amphitheater.
  ID: 18 | Value: An annual classical music festival held in the historic Theatre de Annaba, attracting orchestras and soloists from Algeria, France, Italy, and beyond. Over five evenings, concerts ranging from chamber music to full orchestral performances fill the beautifully restored art deco theatre. The event honors Annaba's deep connection to Saint Augustine, one of Western civilization's great philosophical and musical thinkers.
  ID: 20 | Value: Constantine is the undisputed capital of malouf — the sublime Andalusian-Arabic classical music brought to Algeria by Moorish refugees from Spain in the 15th century. This annual international festival, held in the ornate Palais de la Culture, brings together the finest malouf orchestras and vocalists from Algeria, Tunisia, and Morocco for three evenings of performances. The festival is one of the most important events in North African classical music.
  ID: 22 | Value: A three-day festival celebrating the extraordinary skies of the Sahara, organized in partnership with astronomy associations from Algiers and France. Participants camp under the stars in the desert outside Djanet, guided by expert astronomers with high-powered telescopes. Daytime activities include guided walks to prehistoric rock art sites, Tuareg music performances around campfires, and storytelling sessions about Tuareg celestial navigation traditions.

  ID: 57 | Value: A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1992, La Casbah is the ancient heart of Algiers — a dense, labyrinthine medina tumbling down a steep hillside toward the sea. Built over centuries of Berber, Ottoman, and Andalusian influence, its narrow winding alleys are flanked by tall whitewashed houses with ornate wooden balconies, hidden courtyards, and centuries-old mosques. Walking through La Casbah is like stepping into a living museum: the scent of jasmine mingles with the aroma of freshly baked bread, and the sounds of daily life echo off ancient stone walls. Key landmarks within include the Ketchaoua Mosque, the Palace of the Dey (Dar Hassan Pacha), and numerous Ottoman-era palaces. It remains one of North Africa's most remarkable and atmospheric urban spaces.
  ID: 58 | Value: Perched dramatically on a cliff above the sea in the Bouzaréah hills, Notre-Dame d'Afrique is one of the most striking landmarks of Algiers. Built between 1858 and 1872 in a Romano-Byzantine style, the basilica is adorned with intricate mosaics and its iconic black Madonna statue, above which reads the inscription 'Notre-Dame d'Afrique, priez pour nous et pour les musulmans' — a remarkable testament to Algeria's spirit of coexistence. The panoramic views of Algiers Bay from the basilica terrace are simply breathtaking, especially at sunset when the city glows gold against the Mediterranean.
  ID: 59 | Value: Dominating the Algiers skyline from its hilltop perch, the Maqam Echahid (Martyrs' Memorial) is the most iconic monument of modern Algeria. Built in 1982 to commemorate the 20th anniversary of independence, it consists of three massive concrete palms rising 92 meters into the sky, sheltering a central flame beneath. The structure blends modernist architecture with traditional North African symbolism — the three palms represent the three historic periods of Algeria: the colonial era, the revolution, and independence. At the base, bronze sculptures depict soldiers of the liberation war. The surrounding park offers sweeping views over Algiers and the bay, making it a beloved gathering spot for locals and a pilgrimage site for visitors.
  ID: 60 | Value: Housed in an exquisite 18th-century Ottoman palace surrounded by lush gardens, the Bardo National Museum is a treasure house of Algerian prehistory and cultural heritage. Its collections span tens of thousands of years — from prehistoric rock art of the Sahara and Neolithic artifacts to Berber jewelry, traditional costumes, and domestic objects from across the country's diverse regions. The museum itself is as remarkable as its contents: ornate tilework, carved stucco, and painted ceilings make every room a visual delight. It offers a fascinating journey through the deep human history of Algeria, long before written records.
  ID: 61 | Value: An elegant restaurant in the heart of the city, Le Bosphore serves refined takes on Algerian classics alongside Mediterranean dishes. The couscous royale — piled high with lamb, chicken, merguez, and seasonal vegetables — is legendary among regulars. The terrace overlooking the boulevard is perfect for a long, leisurely lunch.
  ID: 62 | Value: Hidden inside a restored Ottoman house in La Casbah, Dar Zitoun is a true culinary gem. Low wooden tables, zellige tile walls, and the scent of saffron and ras el hanout transport you to another era. The menu focuses on authentic Algerian recipes passed down through generations — mechoui, chakhchoukha, and their legendary rechta noodles.
  ID: 63 | Value: With a prime position on the seafront, La Méditerranée specializes in the freshest local seafood — red mullet, sea bass, and shrimp straight from the Bay of Algiers. The décor is nautical-chic with white linen and blue accents. Their fish tagine with preserved lemon and olives is considered one of the best in the capital.
  ID: 64 | Value: A beloved local institution that has been serving generous Algerian home cooking since the 1970s. Busy, loud, and wonderfully authentic, it's the place locals go for a proper bowl of harira on a cold morning or a hearty midday plate of loubia. Cash only, no reservations — just show up and join the queue.
  ID: 65 | Value: Algiers' grande dame of hospitality, the El Aurassi has dominated the city's skyline since 1975. This five-star landmark offers panoramic views of Algiers Bay from nearly every room, elegant suites decorated with Algerian artisan crafts, multiple restaurants, a rooftop pool, and a full spa. The service is impeccably formal and the Sunday brunch is a social event in itself.
  ID: 67 | Value: Algiers Marriott Hotel Bab Ezzouar is a modern 5-star luxury hotel located in the heart of the Bab Ezzouar business district in Algiers, just a few minutes from Houari Boumediene International Airport. The hotel combines elegant contemporary design with premium comfort, making it ideal for both business travelers and tourists looking for a high-end stay in the capital.
  ID: 68 | Value: A boutique four-star property built in traditional Moorish riad style, with a central courtyard fountain, mosaic tilework, and carved stucco archways. Only 24 rooms, each individually decorated with Algerian antiques. The hammam and spa use locally sourced argan oil and ghassoul clay for treatments.
  ID: 70 | Value: One of the finest examples of Ottoman-Algerian religious architecture, the Great Mosque of Oran was built in 1796 under Bey Mohammed El Kebir. Its elegant minaret rises above the old city quarter, and the interior is a masterpiece of zellige tilework, carved stucco, and chandelier-lit prayer halls. The surrounding neighborhood still retains much of its 18th-century character, with covered market lanes and traditional craftsmen.
  ID: 72 | Value: An opulent 18th-century Ottoman palace, Dar El Senaa was the residence of the Beys who governed western Algeria. Its architecture is a splendid fusion of Ottoman, Andalusian, and Moorish styles: multi-columned reception halls, intricate mosaic floors, carved cedar wood ceilings, and a serene inner garden. The palace was a center of power, culture, and patronage for nearly a century, and today it houses a museum of regional artifacts, historic weapons, and documents relating to the Ottoman period in western Algeria.
  ID: 73 | Value: A legendary Oran institution housed in a restored art nouveau building near the old train station. Le Terminus has been serving faithful customers for decades with its unwavering formula: generous French-Algerian cuisine, excellent house wine, and a convivial atmosphere. Their escalope Oranaise — breaded veal with tomato and olive sauce — is the city's unofficial signature dish.
  ID: 76 | Value: Oran's premier five-star luxury property, the Méridien commands spectacular views of the Mediterranean from its clifftop location. With 250 rooms and suites, a large outdoor pool, a world-class spa, and multiple dining outlets, it sets the standard for luxury hospitality in western Algeria. The rooftop bar is the city's most glamorous cocktail destination.
  ID: 77 | Value: A beautifully restored colonial-era hotel occupying a prime position on the main square, the Grand Hôtel d'Oran blends art nouveau architecture with contemporary Algerian interior design. High ceilings, mosaic floors, and a spectacular central staircase greet guests. The rooftop terrace offers views over the square and old city.
  ID: 79 | Value: Looming directly above the city, the Gouraya Mountain and its national park form one of Algeria's most spectacular natural settings. Declared a national park in 1984, Gouraya protects dense cedar and oak forests, dramatic limestone cliffs plunging into the sea, hidden coves accessible only by boat, and a rich wildlife including the rare Barbary macaque monkey. A road winds up to the fort and summit, offering vertiginous panoramic views of the entire Gulf of Béjaïa, the city, and the coastline stretching to the horizon. The park has numerous hiking trails of varying difficulty and is a paradise for birdwatchers and nature lovers.
  ID: 80 | Value: Standing at the tip of Gouraya's headland like a stone sentinel over the sea, Fort Moussa is a layered historical monument that was built, destroyed, and rebuilt by successive rulers — Romans, Hammadids, Spanish, Ottomans, and French. The current structure dates largely from the Ottoman period and the French colonial era. Its walls enclose the ruins of multiple periods of occupation, and the views from its ramparts over the sea and the city below are among the most dramatic in Algeria.
  ID: 81 | Value: A wild and windswept headland at the edge of the Gouraya massif, Cap Carbon is one of the most dramatic natural landmarks on the Algerian coast. The cape's lighthouse — perched atop sheer cliffs that drop directly into the Mediterranean — has guided sailors for over a century. The surrounding area is a labyrinth of rocky trails, sea caves, and hidden pebble beaches accessible only to those willing to scramble. The view from the cliff edge, looking out at the infinite blue Mediterranean with the forested mountains behind, is genuinely awe-inspiring.
  ID: 84 | Value: A warmly decorated restaurant dedicated entirely to the traditional cuisine of the Kabyle people — one of Algeria's most distinctive and underexplored food cultures. Dishes are made using ancestral recipes: azeqqi (dried figs and nuts), tisilt (barley couscous), and lamb spit-roasted over olive wood. The handmade pottery, Kabyle carpets, and embroidered tablecloths make the dining room feel like an ethnographic museum.
  ID: 90 | Value: Annaba is blessed with some of the finest beaches in Algeria, and the corniche — a winding coastal road flanked by pine trees and golden sandy beaches — is where the city comes to breathe. The stretch between the Seraïdi resort hills and the city beaches is particularly stunning, with clear turquoise water and rocky headlands. The beach at Chapuis and the long sandy expanse of El Battah are especially popular, while the corniches road offers a picturesque drive or cycle at any hour.
  ID: 91 | Value: One of Annaba's most beloved restaurants, Le Phenix has been feeding the city since the 1980s. Specializing in local seafood caught fresh from the Gulf of Annaba and served with traditional Algerian sauces, their signature dish — red mullet stuffed with chermoula and roasted in the wood oven — is the stuff of local legend.
  ID: 92 | Value: Located near the Hippo Regius ruins, La Pergola serves upscale Algerian and Mediterranean food in a shaded garden setting that feels like a Roman villa. Perfect for a long post-ruins lunch. Lamb tagines, homemade pasta, and a remarkable vine-shaded terrace make it a memorable experience.
  ID: 97 | Value: The greatest architectural masterpiece of Ottoman Algeria, the Palais du Bey Ahmed was completed in 1835 for Ahmed Bey ben Mohamed Chérif — the last Ottoman ruler of Constantine — and is a staggering display of wealth, artistry, and political ambition. Over 20 years in construction, using craftsmen brought from across the Ottoman Empire, Andalusia, and Italy, the palace is a vast complex of interconnected courtyards, marble fountains, gilded reception halls, hammams, stables, and gardens. The tilework, carved stucco, painted ceilings, and inlaid marble floors are without equal in Algerian civil architecture. Today it functions as a national museum and remains one of the most visited monuments in Algeria.
  ID: 98 | Value: Named after the ancient Numidian name for Constantine, the Musée Cirta is one of Algeria's finest museums, occupying a grand neoclassical building in the city center. Its collections are remarkably diverse: ancient Numidian and Roman artifacts from the region (including stunning bronze sculptures and Punic stelae), Islamic art and manuscripts, Ottoman-era weapons and costumes, and a significant collection of North African painting from the 19th and 20th centuries. The museum provides essential context for understanding Constantine's extraordinary 2,500-year history.
  ID: 100 | Value: The finest address in Constantine for authentic local cuisine, Le Bey Gourmand is named for the last Ottoman Bey of the city. The restaurant is housed in a beautifully restored Ottoman townhouse, and the menu is a love letter to Constantinois culinary tradition: mutton couscous with seven vegetables, shkhina (slow-cooked wheat berries and meat), and the legendary Constantine-style baklawa — layered with honey and pistachio and unlike anything found elsewhere in Algeria.
  ID: 101 | Value: Without question the most dramatically located restaurant in Algeria, the Panoramic du Gouffre is built literally at the edge of the Rhumel gorge, with a glass wall and wraparound terrace suspended over the abyss. The food — modern takes on Algerian and Mediterranean classics — is excellent, but the view is the real star: 200 meters straight down to the river, with swifts wheeling past your table.
  ID: 106 | Value: Among the most photographed natural formations in all of Africa, the Tin Akachaker arch is a massive sandstone natural bridge rising from the desert plateau with a span of over 30 meters. At sunrise and sunset, the arch glows in shades of deep orange, red, and gold against the limitless blue of the Saharan sky. Reaching it requires a full-day 4x4 journey across spectacular desert terrain — but the sight of this ancient stone arch, carved by wind and time, standing alone in an infinite landscape, is one of the defining experiences of travel in Algeria.
  ID: 110 | Value: A simple, honest restaurant that serves reliable Algerian food to travelers arriving from or departing on long desert expeditions. The harira is thick and restorative, the merguez is grilled to perfection, and the mechanically-simple but deeply satisfying couscous with braised lamb has fueled countless Saharan adventures. A great place to eat before a three-day trek into Tassili.

Dramatic suspension bridges arch over gorges hundreds of meters deep, connecting neighborhoods perched on sheer cliff faces. This is Algeria's most remote and most breathtaking destination.
